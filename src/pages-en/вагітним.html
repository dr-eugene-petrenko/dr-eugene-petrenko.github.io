Safe and caring pregnancy and birth

A page for those preparing for pregnancy, expecting a baby or who have recently become parents. Here you'll find reliable, evidence-based information in plain language.
The information on this page does not replace a consultation with a doctor.

My approach

🛡️ Safety

Decisions are based on current international and Ukrainian guidelines. The goal is not unnecessary interventions, but constantly weighing risks and benefits.

🤍 Love and respect

You are a partner in decision-making. I explain what is happening and why, so that you feel calm and confident.

📚 Evidence

Only what has proven benefit. No myths, no scare stories and no "we've always done it this way".

Caesarean section is an important operation and, today, a safe one when it is indicated. Without medical indications it should be limited: "everything is a medicine, everything is a poison".

Preparing for pregnancy

Preconception consultation

  • Examination and review of chronic conditions and the medicines you take
  • Checking vaccinations (rubella, chickenpox, etc.)
  • Discussing lifestyle, weight and diet

Folic acid

Usually 400 mcg daily — from at least one month before conception until 12 weeks of pregnancy. A higher dose is prescribed by a doctor when indicated.

No alcohol or smoking

There is no safe amount of alcohol in pregnancy. Quitting smoking applies to both partners.

I'm pregnant

Regular visits

WHO recommends at least 8 contacts with a doctor or midwife during pregnancy. Every visit is a chance to ask questions.

Diet and exercise

  • A varied diet and enough water
  • Coffee — up to 200 mg of caffeine a day (about 2 cups)
  • Moderate activity if there are no contraindications

Medicines

Do not start or stop medicines on your own — discuss even "safe" products with your doctor.

⚠️ When to seek care immediately

  • Vaginal bleeding or leaking amniotic fluid
  • The baby is moving less or the pattern of movements has changed
  • Severe headache, spots in front of the eyes, sudden swelling
  • Severe or constant abdominal pain, fever
  • Regular contraction-like pains before 37 weeks

👶 Your baby's movements: how to keep track and why it matters

Movements are your baby's way of "telling" you that all is well. When a baby is short of oxygen or nutrition through the placenta, it often starts to move less. About half of women whose baby died late in pregnancy had noticed reduced movements beforehand. That is why seeking help promptly can save a life.

📅 When and how

  • First movements — usually at 16–24 weeks (earlier in a later pregnancy)
  • From about 24 weeks your baby develops its own pattern of activity and sleep
  • Movements do not decrease before labour: they may feel different (more rolling, fewer kicks), but they should not become less frequent

🔢 How to count

  • What matters most is knowing your own baby's usual pattern. There is no single daily "normal"
  • The "count to 10" method: at a time when your baby is usually active, lie on your left side and note each movement (kick, roll, swish). Most women feel 10 movements well within 2 hours
  • Write down how long it took. If it usually takes 20–30 minutes and today it takes much longer, that is a reason to seek care
  • Hiccups do not count as movements

🚨 Fewer movements — what to do

  • Call the maternity unit or your doctor straight away, even at night — don't wait until morning or your next appointment
  • Don't try to "wake" the baby with something sweet or cold — this does not check how the baby is
  • Don't check the heartbeat with a home Doppler: hearing a heartbeat does not mean all is well
  • If it happens again, seek care again — every time

🏥 What happens at the maternity unit

  • CTG — a recording of your baby's heart rate for at least 20 minutes
  • An examination, blood pressure, questions about the movements
  • If needed, an ultrasound: the baby's size, amount of fluid, blood flow (Doppler)
  • If all is well, you go home; if not, the next steps are discussed — sometimes an earlier birth

🔍 High-risk pregnancy: how your baby is monitored

Some pregnant women need more frequent and detailed monitoring. It is not a verdict — it is a way to notice a problem in time and choose the safest time and way to give birth.

Who needs closer monitoring

  • High blood pressure or pre-eclampsia
  • Diabetes (pre-existing, or gestational needing treatment)
  • A baby small for dates or with growth restriction
  • Stillbirth or growth restriction in a previous pregnancy
  • Twins or more
  • Age 40+, smoking, a much higher body weight
  • Rhesus disease, placental problems, repeated reduced movements
  • Pregnancy beyond 41 weeks

How the baby is monitored

  • Growth scans — every 2–4 weeks: the baby is measured and plotted on a growth chart, rather than compared with a single "normal" number
  • Doppler — blood flow in the umbilical artery and the baby's brain vessels shows how well the placenta is coping
  • Amount of amniotic fluid
  • CTG — from ~28 weeks, how often depends on the situation
  • For the mother: blood pressure, urine and blood tests and, if needed, pre-eclampsia markers (sFlt-1/PlGF)

What you can do

  • Attend every check-up — even when you feel well
  • Pay attention to your baby's movements every day
  • After 28 weeks, go to sleep on your side
  • Don't smoke, and avoid tobacco smoke
  • Take prescribed medicines (such as aspirin or blood pressure tablets) without breaks
  • Keep the maternity unit's phone number and a plan of where to go to hand

How often you are checked and when to give birth is decided by your doctor individually. The goal is not unnecessary interventions, but timely help exactly when it is needed.

Sources: RCOG Green-top Guideline 57 (reduced fetal movements), RCOG Green-top Guideline 31 (small-for-gestational-age fetus, 2024), NICE NG201 (antenatal care, 2021), ACOG Practice Bulletin 229 (antepartum fetal surveillance, 2021), ISUOG (fetal growth restriction, 2020).

Birth

Support by your side

Having a close person with you during labour is a WHO recommendation that helps you feel calmer.

Upright positions

Moving and comfortable positions in the first stage of labour help both you and your baby.

The first minutes

  • Skin-to-skin contact
  • Delayed cord clamping
  • Breastfeeding within the first hour

After birth

Recovery

Rest, support from family and a follow-up visit to the doctor. Your wellbeing matters just as much as your baby's.

Mood

Sadness and tearfulness in the first days are common. If low mood lasts more than 2 weeks, it is a reason to seek help.

Contraception

Discuss contraception before you leave hospital — there are safe options while breastfeeding too.

🔬 What science says today

A brief summary of what has changed in international recommendations in recent years (WHO, FIGO, ACOG, CDC). This is general information — we discuss your own plan in person.

🌿 Before pregnancy

💉 Check your vaccinations

  • Measles, rubella, chickenpox — before pregnancy: these live vaccines are not given during pregnancy
  • After the rubella vaccine it is advisable to wait 1 month

🩺 Chronic conditions — under control

  • Blood pressure, blood sugar, thyroid: the better they are controlled before conception, the safer the pregnancy
  • Review any medicines you take regularly with your doctor in advance
🤰 During pregnancy

💉 Vaccines that protect your baby

  • Whooping cough — at 27–36 weeks in every pregnancy
  • RSV — a single dose at 32–36 weeks: protects the newborn from severe infection in the first 6 months
  • Flu — at any stage of pregnancy during flu season

💊 Aspirin — only when prescribed

  • If a high risk of pre-eclampsia is found in the first trimester, low-dose aspirin substantially reduces it
  • It is best started before 16 weeks — but only on your doctor's advice

🍬 Glucose test

  • For all pregnant women — at 24–28 weeks
  • Earlier if there are risk factors (excess weight, family history of diabetes, previous gestational diabetes)

🩺 Blood pressure of 140/90 or higher

  • This calls for treatment, not waiting: recent studies have shown that treatment lowers the risk of complications
  • Headache, spots in front of the eyes, facial swelling — seek care straight away
👶 Birth

🗓 Induction at 39 weeks

  • For a healthy woman with an uncomplicated pregnancy, a safe option that does not increase the risk of caesarean section
  • It is a decision we make together

🩸 Bleeding after birth

  • New international WHO/FIGO guidance (2025): care starts earlier — already at a blood loss of 300 ml with changes in pulse or blood pressure
  • The team follows a single algorithm — this makes birth safer
🤱 After birth

🍼 Breastfeeding

  • Breast milk only for the first 6 months
  • Then together with solid foods until 2 years and beyond, as long as you and your baby are comfortable

💛 Mood matters

  • Tearfulness in the first 2 weeks is common
  • If sadness lasts longer or it is hard to cope, it may be depression. It is very treatable, and new medicines are becoming available. Tell your doctor

⏳ The next pregnancy

  • WHO advises planning it at least 2 years after giving birth
  • We discuss contraception before you leave hospital
🌸 Women's health

🔬 HPV test instead of a "regular smear"

  • After 30 — an HPV test every 5 years
  • In some countries you may collect the sample yourself — it is just as accurate

💉 HPV vaccine

  • Protects against cervical cancer
  • WHO: between 9 and 20 years of age, a single dose is enough

🌡 Menopause

  • Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flushes, and for most women under 60 it is safe
  • New non-hormonal medicines are now available — for those who cannot take hormones

😣 Painful periods are not normal

  • A possible cause is endometriosis
  • Today it can be diagnosed without surgery — based on symptoms, examination and ultrasound
